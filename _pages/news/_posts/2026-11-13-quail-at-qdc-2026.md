Namasi recently attended IBM’s Quantum Developer Conference (QDC), where he presented a poster on his research so far: HADOF, a scalable quantum optimisation framework, and its application to genome assembly.

This year’s QDC focused on industry and applications, and IBM billed 2026 as "the year of quantum advantage." Early signs of quantum advantage are starting to appear. Several claims have since been overturned, because they pushed researchers to improve classical algorithms until they beat the new quantum baselines. IBM’s Quantum Advantage Tracker records these back-and-forth results, and a range of resource-estimation techniques are being used to project where and when quantum advantage will become achievable.

A highlight was seeing the new ibm_chicago device. It had launched only a week earlier, in IBM’s new office building, which had also just opened.

"Every talk felt relevant to my work,” Namasi said. “Some covered tools I can bring straight into my codebase, like methods for scalability and error mitigation and correction. Others pointed to new applications for my research." 

IBM’s own sessions laid out the company’s roadmap and predictions, along with the new software it is developing, and there were hands-on tutorials too. "It felt like they were aligning everyone around a shared path to quantum advantage. That’s really valuable in a field this new, where a lot of people are still working out where things are headed."

Namasi said the networking was just as valuable. "These are the people I expect to keep running into throughout my research career. They’re the faces of the field, now and in the future."
